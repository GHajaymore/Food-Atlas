## The architecture change that matters

**The catalogue is fetched, not bundled.** `src/data/catalogue.ts` used to `import`
four JSON files; Metro inlines a static import, so 16,000 records of prose became
24 MB of JavaScript and 20–30 seconds of parsing before first paint.

- `src/data/build.ts` — the transformation. All the rules. This is what the tests exercise.
- `src/data/catalogue.ts` — live bindings plus `loadCatalogue()`.
- `public/data/*.json` — what is served. Regenerate with `node scripts/compact-data.mjs`
  then copy `src/data/*.min.json` over `public/data/*.json`.
- `app/_layout.tsx` gates every route on the load, so the seven screens still read
  `catalogue` as a plain array.

**Native is not finished.** A device has no neighbouring `public/` folder.
`EXPO_PUBLIC_DATA_URL` supplies the origin there; web needs nothing.

## The two habits that found almost every bug

**1. Dry-run a rule before applying it.** Every deletion rule was run with `--dry`
first and the output read. This caught: `brand` prefix-matching "(Brandade de
Morue)" and "(brandy)"; "Culture of…" nearly deleting the UNESCO borscht record; an
"X of Y" rule about to remove 282 real provinces (Province of Chieti, Autonomous
Republic of Crimea).

**2. Read the app, not the data.** Three faults this session were invisible in JSON
where every field was individually true:
- The Italian cookbook told Italy its own cookbook was foreign to it.
- The place picker listed China and People's Republic of China as two countries.
- A register record printed "Nothing documents how this is made" above 899
  characters of its own production method.

## The recurring bug class — now covered by a test

Five times a script wrote something correct and the app ignored it. `sourceLanguage`
hard-coded to `'en'` in two mappings; ingredients read only from `evidence`;
`atRiskEvidence` re-derived from the recipe instead of the history; `originClaims`
written as strings into a field typed for sourced claims.

`__tests__/plumbing.test.ts` traces each field from the source row to the record
built from it. **Its first version could not fail** — it asked whether *any* record
showed a field, so deleting the at-risk plumbing left it green. It traces per record
now; reintroducing either bug turns it red, which was verified by doing exactly that.

**Dead ends, recorded so nobody repeats them:**
- **EU DOP/IGP register** — I said it was "a single download". It is not. eAmbrosia
  and GIview are HTML applications with no JSON; the only machine-readable EU data is
  map geometry of production areas.
- **Sicily's PAT file** — server refuses connections outright, not a timeout.
- **Social media photographs** — no free tier, and a credit line is not a licence.
  The lawful free route is the contributor publishing to Wikimedia Commons, which is
  what the contribute flow now asks for.
- **Matching a recipe to a Wikipedia article of the same name.** Measured first: 18%
  of photoless recipes have an article of exactly that title carrying a lead image.
  Then checked what it returned — 3 of the first 6 were wrong. "Arisa" (Malaysian
  blended chicken) matched an Italian singer photographed at Sanremo; "Arambasici"
  (Croatian) matched a Turkish dish; "Arjoli" (Maltese) followed a redirect to French
  aioli. An exact title match is not evidence of the same subject, and a redirect
  actively destroys it — MediaWiki says two names are one subject, not that two
  dishes are one dish. Abandoned; the six photographs were reverted and the script
  deleted rather than left as a trap.

  The mistake was in the probe, not the code: it measured *availability* and not
  *accuracy*. It answered "is there a picture" when the question was "is it the right
  picture". Any future source should be sampled for correctness before it is built.

- **~4,100 Italian PAT products** have no article in any language. One in eight has
  one anywhere. No amount of encyclopaedia scraping reaches them.

## Wikimedia, practically

One rate limit across every Wikimedia API, per IP. **Read `Retry-After`** — it says
four seconds. Guessing an escalating backoff made a four-second pause look like a
permanent outage and cost an hour of misdiagnosis plus six lost batches. Run one pass
at a time.

Two encoding traps, both real: Wikimedia appends UTF-8 tracking parameters to image
URLs (`Name.jpg?utm_source=…`), and MediaWiki returns titles with spaces where URLs
use underscores. Either one silently loses most of a run.

## What is left

**Needs a decision, not code:** translation is inert. `EXPO_PUBLIC_TRANSLATION_ENDPOINT`
is unset, so 5,108 non-English accounts can be identified but not read by someone who
does not speak the language. It needs a backend route holding an API key and a monthly
cap — the only part of this project that costs money.

**Free and available:**
- 3,657 recipes still have no photograph.
- `enrich-infobox` has rows it has not walked.
- Slow Food's Ark of Taste (~6,000 endangered foods) remains the best at-risk source
  and needs a partnership, not a scraper.

# 2026-08-19 — the name, the ask, and the money

## WikiFoodia

Renamed from *Global Taste*. The name now says what the project is — open,
collective, encyclopaedic — so the tagline stopped repeating it and says the thing
the name does not: **"How the world actually cooks."** Most food writing gives you a
convenient version; this one tries to give you what people really do, and "actually"
is the word carrying that.

Two taglines were rejected for a reason worth keeping. *"Written down by the people
who cook it"* describes a community model with no contributions in it yet. *"Before
it's lost"* leads with preservation, which is 31 records of 16,550. A tagline should
not promise what the atlas has not got.

The rename reaches `app.json` (name, slug, scheme, bundle ids) and `src/brand.ts`.
The **folder is still `global-taste`** and so is the git remote — renaming either
buys nothing and breaks paths. One live trap: the preview launch config lives in the
*Golf* project's `.claude/launch.json`, not this repo's, because the tool reads the
primary working directory; its entries are now `wikifoodia-web` / `wikifoodia-dist`.
The Expo dev server reads `app.json` **once at startup**, so a server left running
across the rename serves the old `<title>` and looks like a bug that is not there.

## Donations, and why Open Collective

A donation page is where a free project usually starts lying. "Support our servers"
is the standard line and it is false here — this runs inside a free tier and spends
nothing. An app that deletes fabricated view counts cannot then invent a budget to
ask against.

So `app/support.tsx` names three things and one of the numbers is zero: translation
(tens of dollars a month, and genuinely blocked on it), hosting (nothing so far),
the sources (nothing, and it will stay nothing). Then it says what money does *not*
buy — no badge, no ranking, no advertising — because the product's whole claim is
that classifications come from evidence, and a reader who has just been asked for
money is entitled to hear it.

**Open Collective** was chosen for the same reason the page exists: it publishes the
ledger, itemised and public, so that promise is kept by the platform rather than by
us remembering to update a paragraph. The screen links straight to `/transactions`.

Two decisions in the code that are the point of it:

- The destination is built from a **slug**, not a pasted URL, so there is one place
  it can be wrong and it is obvious when it is.
- With no slug, **there is no button** — `canAcceptDonations()` is false and the card
  says so. A donate control pointing nowhere spends a reader's goodwill on a dead
  link, which is worse than not asking.

It is inert until Ajay creates the collective and sets `EXPO_PUBLIC_OPENCOLLECTIVE`.
I do not create accounts on his behalf.

Currency is **USD**, named once in `CURRENCY` and printed in the lead rather than
written into each sentence — a page about being straight with money should not end up
quoting two currencies and meaning neither.

## Still open

The request *"use public assistant to get the authenticity of the food"* is
unresolved. A free model **judging** authenticity contradicts the rule the whole
catalogue rests on: `assess()` never infers technique or community validation,
because a model guessing "this looks authentic" produces a confident number with
nothing behind it. A model **finding and reading open sources** is a different thing
and is fine. Ajay has not said which he meant.

# 2026-08-20 — find the sources, don't let it judge

Ajay settled the open question from yesterday: an assistant may **find and read real
sources**; it may not **judge** whether a food is authentic. That line is now enforced
in code rather than remembered.

## `src/domain/sourceFinding.ts`

The rules a candidate source must pass, and — more importantly — the ceiling on what
one is allowed to contribute.

**Corroborate, not merely match.** A record already knows a country, sometimes a
region, a cuisine, some ingredients. A real source mentions at least one of them
independently. A text that names the dish and nothing else is not evidence it is the
same dish; it is evidence that two things share a word. This project has paid for that
lesson twice, most expensively when searching Commons by name illustrated a Malaysian
chicken dish with an Italian singer at Sanremo.

**An allow-list of what a finder may write**: a citation, `hasAccount`, ingredients as
text. Never a heritage designation, never the technique or community dimensions —
`assess()` keeps both at zero because nobody in the chain watched anyone cook.

That containment is what makes the thing safe to run at scale, and it is asserted by a
test rather than promised in a comment: the strongest a found source can be is
`hasAccount`, and a record with an account and nothing else is **Unverified with no
score**. A false positive can add a wrong citation. It cannot make the atlas call a
food authentic.

Scripts import this module directly — Node 24 strips the types — so the finder and the
app apply the same test to the byte. That closes, at the source, the class of bug that
bit five times: a script and an app each holding their own copy of a rule.

## The measurement, which is the useful part

English Wikisource, 77 records searched: **one** candidate survived every rule, and it
was Presidential Proclamation 7235 — a tariff schedule — offered as a source for peanut
butter. It named the dish, it named the United States, it corroborated honestly, and it
says nothing about how anyone makes peanut butter.

So `worthCiting()` now requires a text to describe a preparation at all, which takes
the yield to zero. `find-sources.mjs` is kept as the evidence, not as an enrichment.
Pointing different constants at these rules is cheap; believing a corpus without
measuring it is the mistake.

The route that works has no name-matching in it: a record's Wikidata item links to
articles about **the same entity** in other languages. That is
`enrich-native-language.mjs`, and it is now exhausted — of 1,800 records with no
preparation, 1,047 have no article in any language to read.

## What reading the output found instead

Every account here is lifted from a wiki, so every one passes through a stripper, and a
stripper that misses a case does not crash — it ships prose that opens
`right|thumb|300px|` and then some Hindi.

- **312 records** in the cuisine data, because `[[File:x.jpg|thumb|Caption]]` was
  rewritten by a rule meant for `[[Link|text]]`, keeping everything after the *first*
  pipe. The File namespace is `Berkas` in Indonesian and `Tập tin` in Vietnamese, so a
  list of prefixes was always going to be a list of the editions somebody thought of.
- The second fix matched `300px` and missed `300x300पिक्सेल` and `300x300پ`. Hindi and
  Persian write the pixel width in their own scripts, as they write "thumb" as
  अंगूठाकार and بندانگشتی.
- So the guard is **structural, not lexical**: running prose contains no pipe, bracket,
  brace or bare URL, in any language. A handful of genuine leads are refused. No
  account is better than an account that opens with markup.
- **435 records** carried empty brackets — "Хлеб 10 вон ()" — where a template had been
  removed from inside them.
- **1,158 recipes** in the Wikibooks cookbook had the same first-pipe bug in their
  *steps*, which is the product itself: captions glued onto the end of instructions
  ("…lumps start to form in the smooth cream.thumb|Overwhipped cream"), bare reference
  URLs, an unclosed `{{Recipe summary`.

The caption's boundary is gone from the stored text, so `repair-cookbook-markup.mjs`
re-reads those pages from source. Two traps it hit on the way, both of which had
produced confident wrong numbers first: titles from the multilingual ingest carry their
edition in front (`de:Kochbuch/…`), which en.wikibooks treats as an interwiki link and
returns empty; and those pages call their sections Zubereitung and Préparation, not
Procedure. A recipe that re-reads to an empty method is left alone and counted, never
overwritten — a repair that empties a method has deleted a recipe, not fixed one.

## The invariant that should have caught all of it

`findCatalogueViolations` now refuses reader-facing prose containing wiki markup, and
the test runs it **against the whole catalogue** rather than a two-record fixture. The
312 were invisible to 213 passing tests because every test built its own pool, and a
fixture only ever proves the fixture. It found the cookbook's 1,158 within seconds of
being pointed at real data.

Also fixed while here: `ingest-wikibooks.mjs` replaced whole records on re-run, so
re-reading a page would have deleted its country, photograph, credit and licence — six
later passes' work. It merges now. `notAFood` gained `group|holdings|industries|
enterprises`, which removes 17 beverage corporations (Wahaha, Lotte Holdings, PRAN-RFL)
and nothing else. The native-language pass now skips rows already flagged `notFood`; it
had been reading articles about restaurant chains and taxonomists in 270 cases.

### Where the markup repair finished

- **571** recipes re-read from source with the fixed parser.
- **1,950** Spanish entries fixed in place — that cookbook stores every recipe as one
  template, so `{{ing|Sal` is an ingredient with its brackets eaten, not junk. Taking
  the last pipe segment keeps the name the cook uses: `{{ing|Cebolla|Cebolleta` →
  *Cebolleta*, `फूलगोभी {{small` → *फूलगोभी*. Nothing was renamed and nothing emptied.
- **370** lines dropped as unrecoverable: an image caption glued to a step has no
  boundary left in the stored text. 160 recipes kept a usable method; **3** lost theirs
  entirely and are now name-and-place records, which the app already says plainly.
- **14** prose fields truncated at the markup boundary, backed off to the last full
  sentence; 7 kept their prose, 7 were too short to keep and were cleared.
- **61** recipes were left untouched because re-reading produced no method at all. A
  repair that empties a method has deleted a recipe, not fixed one.

**0 of 20,169 records** now carry markup in any reader-facing field, verified against
what the app actually serves rather than against what the scripts believe they wrote.

Two things stay honest about this: 113 native-language leads were *refused* by the new
structural guard, so the preparation count went down before it went up, and the Spanish
cookbook still has no section names — 102 of its recipes cannot be re-read by a section
reader at all, only cleaned in place.

# 2026-08-21 — the rows that were never marked

Looked for what was still unwalked and found something better: a bug that had been
spending requests on the same 145 rows since the day `redirects=1` was added.

## The redirect loop

`enrich-infobox.mjs` asks Wikipedia for a batch of titles with `redirects=1`, then
files each answer under the title it came back with. Ask for **Curry leaves** and the
answer is titled **Curry tree** — so the lookup missed, the row was never patched, and
because these passes mark a row as walked *only* when they patch it, it stayed pending
and was re-fetched on every run since.

It reported as "145 still pending", which reads like work remaining rather than a
fault. That is the tell worth remembering: **a count that never moves is a bug, not a
backlog.** Once unstuck those rows immediately yielded 45 preparations, 35 real places
and 60 ingredient lists that had been sitting in Wikipedia the whole time.

One layer below it, the same loop again: several records share one article. "Rau
muống", "Kangkung (vegetable)" and "Trakuon" are three records here and one page on
Wikipedia, so MediaWiki answers three questions with a single page, and filing it
against only the first left the other two pending forever. `requestedTitles` therefore
returns a *list* per answer, and the patch is computed per row — two records sharing
an article need not share a country.

Both now live in `scripts/lib/mediawiki.mjs`, because the moment a second script needed
the rule was the moment to stop copying it. `enrich-places.mjs` and
`ingest-wikibooks.mjs` had the same defect; in the ingest it was worse than a stalled
row, since a missed lookup there would have *created a second record* under the
redirect's target instead of filling in the one that exists.

`enrich-places.mjs`, once fixed, placed **384** more records below country level and
took the atlas from 1,342 to **1,504 distinct places** — the number the coverage screen
is built on, and the one that is supposed to be hard to inflate.

## And the same markup bug, a fourth time

`enrich-infobox.mjs` had its own copy of `[[Link|text]] -> text`, so
`[[File:Sheet pan.jpg|thumb|Large Sheet pan|baking…]]` opened Baklava's blurb. The
multi-row fix above then faithfully copied it to Azerbaijani pakhlava too, which is a
fair description of what that fix does: it spreads whatever the parser produces.

Fixed the same way as the others, and `preparationProse` now applies the structural
guard to its own output — no pipe, bracket, brace or bare URL — rather than trusting
the stripper above it.

Worth recording that one check here was **too** aggressive: a single `[` is legitimate
in recipe prose. "The Brown Derby Cookbook [Doubleday & Company: Garden City NY] 1949"
is a citation, and "180 ml [3/4 tasse] de crème" is a unit conversion a cook wants. The
invariant matches `[[`, not `[`, and 24 apparent problems were an artefact of a quick
check rather than anything wrong in the data.

## What is genuinely exhausted

Measured rather than assumed, and all of it free-corpus work with nothing left in it:

- **Recipe photographs.** All 3,657 photoless recipes have been walked; their pages
  carry no image. The 2,659 rows that looked unwalked already had photographs.
- **Placing recipes by country.** 2,029 cookbook recipes have no country. Their
  Wikibooks categories are technique and theme — "Easy recipes", "Broiled recipes",
  "African recipes" — not countries. A few are tantalising (`Tiv recipes` is a Nigerian
  people) but that needs an ethnicity map, and "African" is a continent, which
  `place.ts` refuses on purpose.
- **Native-language accounts.** Zero pending.
- **Infobox and risk.** Zero pending, for the first time.

The remaining gap is 883 records with no article in any language and no method
anywhere. Nothing free reaches them. That is a contributor problem now, not a scraper
problem.

## Then: reading the front page

With the scrapers exhausted, the useful thing left was to look at the app. Four faults
were visible on the first screen, and the biggest was in the position the atlas
reserves for its best evidence.

**UNESCO inscriptions were being used as dish names.** UNESCO titles the *practice*, so
the listing for ceviche is called "Practices and meanings associated with the
preparation and consumption of ceviche, an expression of Peruvian traditional cuisine".
That was the card title. `src/domain/inscription.ts` now lifts the dish out of the
inscription — the official title stays as the record's source, which is what the reader
follows to UNESCO, so nothing is lost.

**And a third of those inscriptions are not food.** Among the sixty-four: a livestock
market, a seasonal cattle drive, several saints' feasts, a fishing rite, and
"Safeguarding the carillon culture", which is church bells. Every one was showing as
Authentic — Regional at 62/100. They are refused now, with a reason each, in the shape
`isDish` and `place` already use. Twenty-seven refused, thirty-seven kept.

The rule is patterns plus an explicit refusal list rather than anything clever, because
the failure mode of guessing here is a confident wrong dish name on the most prominent
card in the app. It is deliberately *not* over-eager: "Ceremonial Keşkek tradition" is
keşkek, a wheat and meat dish, and a rule that refused it for its first word would be
the same mistake in the other direction.

**1,368 names arrived lower-case.** Wikidata labels common nouns that way — "popcorn",
"pea soup", "chimichurri" — and beside "Kozhikode Halwa" they read as a bug. Only the
first letter is touched, and only when it is a lower-case letter, so a name in a
non-Latin script is returned unchanged.

**Five regions were hedges, not places.** `place_of_origin` sometimes says "Various
claims" or "Primarily Central Europe", which the infobox reader truncated to "Primarily
Central" and printed under Kompot where a reader expects a town.

## The count that was inflated by thirty-two

Sentence-casing surfaced the real one. "falafel" became "Falafel" and met the Falafel
already in the atlas — two records, one filed under Egypt and one under "Middle Eastern
empires", which is not a country.

Seventy-nine records were filed under an origin that is not a country: Levant, Asia,
Mesoamerica, the Maghreb, the Ottoman Empire, the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, and
twenty-seven more. Each was counted as one, which is why the Elsewhere group said "32
countries" and the headline said **202**. The real number is **170**.

What was *not* done matters as much. None of those origins was narrowed to a modern
state: choosing which country the Levant is would be exactly the argument this atlas
refuses to settle for a reader. Sixty of the records are the only entry for their dish —
gefilte fish, kugel, aşure, popcorn — and a broad origin is a fact about a dish, not a
reason to delete it. They are kept, shown under "Elsewhere", and that group now says
"27 origins" instead of claiming they are countries.

Nineteen were genuine duplicates: the same dish, once under a country and once under
something vaguer. Those fold into the placed record.

The first attempt at `isCountry` tested `CONTINENTS.has(name)` and changed nothing on
screen, because `registerContinents` adds every pair the import carries — including
`["Levant", "Elsewhere"]`. A membership test said yes to precisely the values it was
meant to exclude. It tests for a real continent now.

One deliberate non-decision: 101 of 571 dish names appear in both the cuisine tree and
the Wikidata rows under **different** countries, and some of those disagreements are
real traditions rather than errors. No winner is picked. Cuisine rows simply lead the
ordering, on the grounds that they carry an article, an infobox and a preparation where
a Wikidata row carries a label — a judgement about documentation, not about origin.
Both records survive and both origin claims stay visible.

## Reading the dish pages, then the form

Two faults on the record pages, both found by looking rather than by testing.

**The UNESCO score did not add up.** The card printed 62/100 directly above six
dimensions averaging 55. The score was written by hand; the breakdown beside it was
written separately. A reader who added up the numbers the app showed them got a
different answer from the one on the card — which, in an atlas whose whole argument is
that its figures can be checked, is the worst kind of small error.
`findCatalogueViolations` now refuses any record whose score is more than a rounding
point from its own dimensions. It found these thirty-seven and nothing else.

**And twelve thousand records asked readers to confirm a blank.** A page saying
"Nobody has recorded how this is made" then asked "Is this how it's made where you're
from?" over a button marked **Yes — this matches**. There was nothing to match, and
that tap is meant to be evidence. `confirmAsk()` now asks about what the record
actually holds: with no method, the question moves to the place, which someone from
Charente can answer about cagouilles even though nobody has written the recipe down.
It is a real question, and place is one of the six evidence dimensions.

## The form that went nowhere

Then the bigger one. Having established that every free corpus is exhausted — that what
remains is food nobody has written down — the obvious next question was whether the app
can actually accept it. It could not.

`/contribute` was a four-step walkthrough. Step one's fields were `defaultValue`
demonstrations filled in with Kaipola, and nothing typed into them was read by
anything. Steps two and three showed findings and seven checks about *that* example
while a reader's own entry sat unexamined beside them. Step four ended with a green
badge and "Published with its evidence visible" — a sentence about a record that was
never created, shown to someone who had just typed out their grandmother's recipe.

Now:

- the fields are real inputs, with the examples moved to placeholders, because a form
  that submits its own sample data is worse than one that submits nothing;
- the screen says the later steps are a worked example from a record already in the
  atlas — in the app, not only in a comment;
- the last step offers a real destination, or says plainly there is not one yet.

**The destination is a form opened at source**, the same trade `requests.ts` already
made: no server, no database, no monthly bill, no account demanded of the person
submitting. It is a worse experience than capturing in-app and it is the difference
between a feature that exists and one that is switched off. Set
`EXPO_PUBLIC_CONTRIBUTION_FORM_URL` and it works; until then `canContribute()` is false
and there is no button — the donate page's rule, for the donate page's reason.

Three fields are required and the rest are not: dish, place, and the contributor's
connection to the place. That last one is the whole difference between a submission and
a recipe copied off the internet, which is the thing the atlas exists not to do.

## 202 → 170 → 156

The country count came down twice more, and each step removed something that was not
a country.

The first pass caught origins with no continent — Levant, Mesoamerica, the Maghreb. The
place picker then showed what it had missed: **Goryeo**, **Joseon** and **Ottoman
Empire**, sitting under ASIA among the countries a reader can choose. Those are in the
continent map deliberately, and should be: the file says so in as many words — *a dish
is no less placed for having outlived its state* — and without a continent an Ottoman
dish becomes unreachable. But `isCountry` tested for a real continent, and they have
one, so all fourteen historical states carrying records were being counted.

They are listed now as data rather than as a `// Historical` comment, which is a note
to a person. A test asserts both halves at once, because both matter and they pull in
opposite directions: a historical state must have a continent, and must not be a
country.

The honest figure is **156**. The atlas said 202 this morning.

Worth recording that the test I wrote to protect this was itself wrong first: it
asserted `isCountry('Türkiye')` and failed, which looked like a missing country and was
actually the test skipping `canonicalCountry`. The map is keyed by one spelling per
country and every record is canonicalised before it is stored. That contract is now
asserted directly — over the whole catalogue, not a fixture.

## Two smaller things from reading the rest of the app

The search empty state leads with "I know how it's made — record it", and the comment
above it says *the dish name is already typed*. It was not passed. `requests.ts` writes
down exactly why this matters — asking someone to type it twice is the friction that
kills this kind of capture — and had implemented it for asking but not for recording,
which is the path this screen actually leads with. The query now travels as a route
param and the form seeds its first field from it.

And the contribute form's own fields, which had been `defaultValue` demonstrations,
now hold what the reader types — which is what made carrying the name across possible
at all.

## "0% · 44"

Two meters on the coverage screen read `0% · 44` and `0% · 5`. Both numbers were
right; together they read as a contradiction, and most people take the percentage and
conclude there are none. One of them is *Classified as authentic*, which is the figure
a sceptical reader checks first.

`percentLabel` renders a non-zero count that rounds to nothing as **<1%**. Zero stays
`0%`, because zero is a different fact. It applies to the coverage tables too, where
Oceania's thirty records had the same problem.

Everything else on that screen reconciles: the continent rows sum to the total, the
confidence bands sum to the total, and the concentration figure matches Italy's own
count in the picker. Worth stating, since checking it was the point.

## The picker's own honesty

The country picker listed Ancient Greece, Byzantine Empire, Kievan Rus' and the Soviet
Union between Albania and Austria, under a heading reading "Choose a country". They
belong there and should stay — but as peers of Austria they read as a mistake in the
data rather than as a fact about a dish. Each such row now names itself: *former state*
or *wider region*.

And "Kingdom of the Netherlands", which is what Wikidata calls it, was its own entry
under Elsewhere — three Dutch dishes filed outside both the Netherlands and Europe.

## A check that was wrong, caught before it did damage

Added three rules for records that contradict themselves on screen — the class of fault
that once printed "Nothing documents how this is made" above 899 characters of a
record's own method. Two of them found nothing, which is the answer worth having.

The third was wrong, and it is worth recording *how* it was wrong. `types.ts` described
🏺 as "set only where no modern substitutions have been identified", so a rule pairing
the badge against a recorded `adaptation` looked obviously right. It flagged Kozhikode
Halwa, Thalassery Halwa, Neapolitan Pizza Margherita and Hákarl.

Four hand-curated records failing a new rule is not four bad records. The badge
describes *this record's* preparation, which uses nothing modern; `adaptation` names
what other people substitute when an ingredient is unavailable, kept deliberately out
of `ingredients` and shown in its own disclosure labelled as not the authentic version.
Two curated records carry an adaptation without the badge, which settles it: they are
independent.

So the rule went and the comment was rewritten, because the comment was the thing that
was wrong and it would have misled the next reader exactly as it misled this one. The
constraint that does hold — the badge belongs only to authentic records — was already
checked a hundred lines above.

Had the rule been trusted, the "fix" would have been deleting true and useful content
from the four best records in the atlas.

## The rail that never saw the filter

Selecting **Fusion** gives a feed saying "1 tradition worldwide" — and then, below it,
a "Most looked up" rail showing Kopi luwak, Pierogi, Shawarma and Baklava. None of them
is a fusion record. The rail is computed from the whole catalogue and never sees the
active filter.

Narrowing it would have been the obvious fix and the wrong one: it measures readership
on English Wikipedia, so filtering it to Fusion leaves one entry or none, and a rail
about popularity that shows the reader's own query back to them measures nothing.

It is hidden once the reader has narrowed instead — the rule the shelves above it
already follow, for the reason already written there: someone who has picked a country
is past being offered somewhere to start. Same for someone who has picked Fusion.

## A sentence that said the wrong thing about the atlas

Filtering to **vegan** and **breakfast** emptied the feed, and the explanation read:

> Nothing classified as All and vegan anywhere in the atlas.

Two faults in one line. The occasion is missing, so the sentence claims there is no
vegan food in the atlas — there is, and none of it is also breakfast. And "classified as
All" is not a classification; naming the permissive filter as the reason implies a
narrowing the reader never applied.

`narrowingSummary` names every active constraint and drops the filter when it is the
default one. It is in the domain with tests because the fault here is entirely in the
wording, and wording is the part that gets edited without anyone re-checking what it
now claims.

Worth noting what the empty result itself is *not*: a bug. Diet is only classified where
a method is recorded, so "vegan" matches two records out of 16,489. That is the
`assess()` rule working — a dietary claim without a documented preparation is exactly
the kind somebody keeps a vow on — and the empty state says so in the app's own voice.

## Popularity from more sources — measured, then dropped

Ajay asked whether the popularity rail should take the highest hit count from wherever
it can be found, social platforms included. Two questions, two answers.

**Other Wikipedia editions: measured, and it changes nothing.** The objection is
obvious and sound-sounding — kimchi is read about by Koreans, so counting English
readers ranks how interesting a food is to English speakers. It is wrong. Over twelve
months:

    Kimchi     en 489,127   ko  17,280      28×
    Pierogi    en 674,404   pl  35,520      19×
    Sushi      en 589,009   ja  97,857       6×
    Borscht    en 435,413   ru 209,612       2×
    Rendang    en  94,755   id  32,403       3×
    Appam      en  70,728   id   8,687       8×

English is the largest edition in six of six, and those six were chosen as the cases
most likely to disprove it. A trial run over twelve catalogue records found none that
peaked outside English either. Computing the maximum across every edition would cost
about 37,000 API requests and move the least important number on the screen by almost
nothing. `measure-readership-by-language.mjs` is kept as the evidence, not as an
enrichment — the same standing as `find-sources.mjs`.

Worth noting the check that made this trustworthy: the first result was "0 of 12 peaked
outside English", which is exactly what a silently failing request would produce. The
non-English lookups were timed and read back individually before the finding was
believed.

**Social platforms: no.** Three reasons, in order of firmness. There is no free hit
count — Instagram's Graph API needs a business account and app review and does not
report hashtag totals, and TikTok's research API is restricted to accredited
researchers; scraping either breaks their terms, and this project is meant to cost
nothing and stay legal. A hashtag is not a dish: #pierogi counts posts that used a
word, and name-matching is what put an Italian singer on a Malaysian chicken dish. And
the rail is deliberately the least load-bearing thing on the screen — bottom of the
page, never mixed into confidence, labelled for what it is — so elaborating it spends
effort where the app has already decided accuracy matters least.

## The at-risk badge was wrong about half the time

"🕯️ At-Risk Tradition" leads the front page under the heading *Traditions a source
describes as declining. The reason this atlas exists.* Reading the twenty-nine
sentences behind it, roughly half did not describe a decline at all.

**Records flagged over a revival.** `being revived` and `revival of` were in the
IMPLIED list, on the reasoning that something revived must first have declined.
Perhaps — but the sentence is then shown to the reader *as the evidence*, and it said
the opposite of the badge above it. Hoppy carried the badge over "it is still a staple
among some Tokyo residents, and has experienced a retro revival of late". Korean royal
court cuisine over "there has been a revival of this cookery style in the 21st
century". Dalgona over a sentence about a television show making it internationally
popular.

**Records flagged over something else's decline.** This is the recurring shape, and it
is the same one `sourceFinding` was built for: the vocabulary was right and the subject
was wrong. Bosnian pot — *fireplaces* are in decline. Nata de coco — *nata de piña* is
seasonal. Reblochon — an *Italian imitation* sold in declining quantities. "Food during
the occupation of Japan" — "the few remaining items on the bookshelf".

Three changes:

- revival language joins the false friends, alongside the existing business-count
  entries that already refused "a restaurant chain has shrunk" as evidence;
- the counting phrases — `last remaining`, `few remaining`, `only a handful` — moved
  from STATED to IMPLIED, because a count is not a claim;
- **implied language must now be shown to be about the dish.** Every significant word
  of the name has to appear in the sentence. Stated language is exempt: "though once
  common, the knowledge to make the food product is slowly dying out" never says
  *Sendango*, and is exactly the record the feature exists for.

Sixteen flags withdrawn; seventeen stand. Three or four of those are still arguable —
orange chicken is "rarely found in Chinese restaurants in China", which is a claim
about geography rather than survival — and at seventeen the set is now small enough to
read in one sitting, which is the honest place to leave a badge that asserts cultural
endangerment.

The script had its own copy of these lists, kept in step with the domain by hand. They
had drifted, which is how the revival records shipped. It imports the tested rules now.

## Ten thousand photographs, one warning, mostly false

Every imported record carried the same line under its picture:

> Matched by name on Wikimedia Commons — the subject is not confirmed.

It is a good warning. It was true of about three thousand photographs and false for the
other seven thousand, because most pictures in this atlas were never matched by name at
all:

- **3,050** come from the dish's own Wikidata item (P18) — an image somebody attached to
  *that entity*;
- **3,559** are the lead image of the dish's own encyclopaedia article — chosen by
  editors to illustrate *that article*;
- **663** are published on the recipe's own Wikibooks page;
- the rest — Commons name searches, and the UNESCO inscriptions, which search behind a
  plausibility guard — genuinely are matches, and keep the warning.

It is the same distinction the project already draws everywhere else: identity against
resemblance. `enrich-recipe-images.mjs` states it in its own header as the reason to
prefer a page's image over a search, then the app labelled both the same way.

**Under-claiming is not the safe side.** A warning printed on every photograph stops
being read, and the three thousand where it applies are the ones that need it. Crying
unverified on a picture the encyclopaedia chose trains a reader to ignore the label on
the picture a search guessed at.

`photoVerified` stays false on all ten thousand. Knowing an image was attached to the
right subject is not knowing it shows the dish *as made in the place*, which is the
standard the rest of the record is held to — and the curated records, where a person
did check, still say things like "Photographed in Oaxaca, Mexico".

Two of the fields this turns on — `leadFile` and `pageImageChecked` — were sitting in
the plumbing test's BOOKKEEPING list, classified as run-state nobody reads. They were,
right up until the app needed to tell the truth about a photograph.

## Looking at it on a phone, which is what it is for

The app is phone-first and every review so far had been done at 882px. At 375px the
layout holds — no horizontal overflow anywhere, no clipped text, the wide elements are
all inside horizontal rails where they belong.

What does not hold is the size of things to press. Nineteen controls were below the
44px a finger reliably hits, and the list is unflattering:

- the **six authenticity chips** — 22px, half the minimum, and they are the feed's
  primary control on the screen the app opens on;
- **eleven "How is this counted?" toggles** — 14px, on the coverage screen whose entire
  purpose is letting a sceptical reader check the numbers;
- the adaptation disclosure (28px), the Refine row (40px), and "Capture the ingredients
  and steps from this video" (36px), which is the control that turns a viewer into a
  contributor.

`TAP_TARGET = 44` already exists in the tokens and the place picker's rows already use
it. These are the places that missed it.

**`hitSlop` alone would not have fixed it**, and this is worth recording because it is
the idiomatic React Native answer and it looks right in a diff: **react-native-web
ignores it entirely**. Checked rather than assumed — the rendered button has no
pseudo-element and no padding change, and a click 8px above the chip hits nothing. So
the targets are grown structurally too, with padding on an outer pressable and a
negative margin handing the space back to the layout. The visible chip is an inner
view and keeps its 22px, its border and its position to the pixel.

The 11px expansion fits inside the 16px gaps the chip row already sits in, so a larger
target reaches neither the place selector above nor the Refine row below, and the four
horizontal pixels take the shortest chip — "All", 36px — to exactly 44 without meeting
its neighbour across the row's 8px gap.

Every control on the feed, the record, the atlas, the picker and the contribution form
now meets the minimum.

## The badge that had never shown its evidence

`atRisk.ts` opens with a promise:

> Every flag keeps the sentence that produced it. A reader can check the claim, and a
> wrong one is visibly wrong rather than an unexplained badge — the same standard every
> other claim in the app is held to.

`types.ts` repeats it: *"A badge without its evidence is exactly the unexplained
assertion this app refuses to make anywhere else."*

`atRiskEvidence` and `AT_RISK_NOTE` were rendered by **nothing**. Not on the card, not
on the record, nowhere. The field was written by the ingest, asserted to arrive by the
plumbing test, and displayed to no one.

That is why half the flags could be wrong for as long as they were. The evidence was
invisible, so nobody — including this session, until the data was read directly —
could see that Hoppy's sentence was about a revival or that Bosnian pot's was about
fireplaces. **The check the design relied on was the one thing not built.**

It renders now, quoted, under the record's place, with the note explaining what the
flag means and what it is never inferred from.

And it immediately did its job. The first record opened after the change was Dhooska,
whose evidence reads *"Dhooska is mostly made in market-area stalls where people enjoy
it as a snack and is rarely found in larger restaurants"* — a claim about where a dish
is sold, not whether it survives. Orange chicken's says *"rarely found in Chinese
restaurants in China"*, which is about authenticity and geography and describes a dish
in no danger at all. A narrow rule now refuses "in … restaurants" as a menu claim; it
takes four more flags out and touches nothing else.

Fifteen at-risk records stand, from twenty-nine this morning. The difference between
this and the earlier passes is that a reader can now check every one of them.

- **Contrast.** Every rendered text node on the feed, the atlas and a record meets WCAG
  AA against its own computed background. The Nocturne palette holds. (The one failure
  the first sweep reported was the `<title>` element in `<head>`, which is not
  rendered — the check was wrong, not the palette.)
- **Accessible names.** No control anywhere is nameless; every image carries meaningful
  alt text, including the video stills.
- **Duplicates.** The raw sources share 437 name-and-country keys, but the build's

  record each. Measured because the raw number looked alarming; not a bug.

## Thirty per cent of the photographs would have been blank in production

Sampling image URLs to look for dead links found something worse. Twelve of
twenty-five catalogue photographs timed out while the cuisine and cookbook samples
loaded perfectly — and the difference was the scheme.

**All 3,055 photographs from the Wikidata source are stored as `http://`.** Every other
source stores `https://`. Wikidata returns its image property over plain http and
nothing had ever normalised it.

It works flawlessly here, because the dev server is itself http. Served over https —
which is every real deployment — a browser refuses an http image as mixed content and
renders nothing at all. Thirty per cent of the atlas's photographs were going to be
invisible to readers and perfect in every check ever made on this machine.

That is the sharpest example yet of the habit this session keeps relying on: it is not
enough to look at the app, you have to look at the app *the way the reader will get
it*. This one was invisible even to looking, because looking happens over http.

Fixed at the build rather than in the data, because the next Wikidata ingest would put
it straight back. Commons serves the same files over https, so the rewrite costs
nothing. The credit link is rewritten too — it points at the same file. A test asserts
no record ships an `http://` image or credit link.

## The photographers' names

Chasing the http finding through every other URL the data carries turned up the credit
line, which is shown under every photograph and is a **condition of the licence**
rather than a courtesy.

Of 7,583 credits:

- **61** read "No machine-readable author provided. J.P.Lon~commonswiki assumed (based
  on copyright claims)." Kipper's card on the front page carried that sentence as its
  photographer. It is Commons apparatus wrapped around a real username.
- **29** carried undecoded HTML entities, so a reader saw "Canadian National
  Collections &amp; Zhaofu Yang" with the ampersand spelled out.
- **17** carried wiki-link residue — "Raveesh Vyas from [Ahmedabad, Noida], India", and
  a Korean credit ending in a stray bracket where `[url name]` had been half-stripped.

`tidyCredit` fixes presentation and is careful about what it refuses to touch. It does
**not** shorten the 105 credits over eighty characters, some of which are a paragraph:
that is ugly and it is what the photographer asked for. It does not replace the 17 bare
URLs with a domain, because a URL is all Commons holds for those people. And if any
rule would leave the field empty, the original stands — an ugly credit is a licence
met, a missing one is not.

Kipper now reads "Gaius Cornelius · Public domain".

## 283 records were illustrated with something that is not food

Ajay noticed the same picture twice on the front page. It was Thalassery Halwa carrying
Kozhikode Halwa's tray — two curated records, side by side on one rail, one photograph.
The record's own `photoOrigin` admitted the shot was "Malabar halwa" with no location
recorded, and the record exists to say Thalassery's version is *less sugar, set thinner,
cut smaller*. Illustrating it with Kozhikode's shows a reader the opposite, so it now
shows the monogram — which says no photograph is on record, and is true.

Chasing the duplicate found the larger fault. Of 10,295 photographs, **283 are not
photographs of food**:

- **220 records share `Noia_64_apps_energy.png`** — a KDE desktop icon;
- **35 share `ChineseDishLogo.png`**;
- the rest are company logos (Brooke Bond, Bombay Sweets, Mavalli Tiffin Room), a
  wine-region locator map, and two PDFs — one a scanned 1936 book called *Plenty of
  Onions* — which a browser cannot render as an image at all.

They come from articles that use a placeholder graphic where a photograph would go, and
the ingest took the article's image without asking what the image was. `isPhotograph`
refuses them; those records show the monogram, which 49% of the atlas already does. An
icon of a lightning bolt captioned as Andalusian olives is not a photograph a reader is
better off having.

The rule is deliberately narrow. Real photographs are saved as png often enough that
the extension proves nothing — "Baklava(1).png" and "Pizzas Buenos Aires.png" are real
— so only formats a browser will not show are refused outright, and otherwise the file
name has to actually say logo, icon, map, or one of the icon-set prefixes.

Separately, a shelf will no longer show one photograph twice, in either direction:
against what earlier rails used, and against itself. Sibling traditions are the records
most likely to share a picture and exactly the ones a reader is meant to tell apart.

## The front page moves now, and leads with what needs help

Two asks from Ajay, and they pull in the same direction.

**It was static.** Ranking is deterministic, so every visit ever made met the same
twelve cards. An atlas of sixteen thousand traditions that shows the same three every
day is not describing what it holds, and gives a returning reader no reason to come
back.

The rail now rotates, and the constraints on *how* are the design:

- **Over a pool of equals, never the whole shelf.** Records past the strongest few
  railfuls are genuinely thinner — unscored, less documented, worse illustrated — and
  rotating them in would be variety bought by showing a reader the second-best of
  everything. The window is three railfuls; the turn picks today's slice.
- **A day, not a render.** Reshuffling per render would move the card a reader was
  about to tap; per session would mean the back button led somewhere else. A day is
  long enough to be a stable object while you use it and short enough to be worth
  returning to.
- **Each shelf turns by a different amount**, so they do not all change together and
  then all sit still together.

Two shelves cannot rotate and are left alone rather than faked: Disappearing has 15
records and 11 photographs, Authenticated has 12 photographed records to draw on. That
is the coverage story the atlas already tells about itself.

**And the danger case leads with the danger.** Every other rail shows its strongest
record first, because a rail is a shop window. Disappearing is not a shop window — it
is the argument the atlas is built on — and there the *weakest* record is the most
urgent. A tradition a source calls declining, with nothing recorded about how it is
made, is the one that actually goes. Five of the fifteen are in exactly that state, and
they now lead: Salers and Saumagen at 12/100 ahead of Hákarl at 90, which is already
saved in the only way this app can save anything.

The shelf's own copy says so: *"the undocumented ones first, because those are the ones
that go."*

## The credit under a photograph fits now

On a rail card the credit has about 100px. **Thirty of the fifty-one on the front page
did not fit**, so "Manuel González Olaechea · CC BY 3.0" rendered as "Manuel González
Ola…". Truncating an attribution is worse than an untidy one: it credits a photographer
by a name they do not have, on a field that is a condition of the licence rather than a
caption.

Two lines rather than one, and a slightly wider pill. Three of fifty-one still clip, and
those are genuinely long — "Evan Joshua Swigart (TheCulinaryGeek) from Chicago, IL, USA
· CC BY-SA 2.0" is not going to fit on a thumbnail.

`tidyCredit` also learned two more shapes Commons wraps a username in: **41 records**
read "Original uploader was Natto at ja.wikipedia", and one carried a "Later versions
were uploaded by…" clause. Both keep the name and drop the file-history sentence.

`derivative work:` is deliberately left alone. "Chopstick.JPG: 毛抜き derivative work:
